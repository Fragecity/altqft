\begin{abstract}
The hidden subgroup problem (HSP) yielding exponential quantum speedup over the best known classical algorithms, uses the Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT). Standard HSP algorithms require the QFT, whose circuit depth and long-range gates remain challenging for near-term hardware. We propose a departure from conventional QFT approximations: instead of mimicking its gate structure, we identify two functional properties that are exploited by the QFT to enable HSP. Firstly the shift invariance property of the QFT allows for the removal of a random overall shift. Secondly, the information about the hidden subgroup generator needs to remain accessible in the computational basis, and we quantify that information via the Fisher information. We construct a family of shallow circuits that preserve shift invariance. Numerical analysis shows these circuits retain Fisher information.  We demonstrate successful reconstruction using a neural network and analyze performance under noise, outlining a potential path to practical HSP implementations.
\end{abstract}

The exponential quantum speedups demonstrated by landmark protocols—such as Shor's algorithms for prime factorization and discrete logarithms \cite{Shor1994, Shor1997}, as well as Simon's algorithm \cite{Simon1997} use the quantum Fourier transform (QFT). The associated family of algorithms is known as the (quantum) hidden subgroup problem (HSP) algorithms \cite{Kitaev1995, Jozsa2001, Lomont2004}. The $\mathcal{O}(n^{2})$ depth complexity of the QFT remains a critical bottleneck for HSP algorithms. For a $100$-qubit system ($n=100$), the QFT demands thousands of circuit layers, which vastly exceeds current hardware capabilities. For instance, IBM's $127$-qubit Eagle processor is limited to a depth of $60$ layers even with extensive error mitigation \cite{kim2023evidence}, and Google's $105$-qubit Willow processor reaches its operational threshold at around $40$ layers~\cite{google2025quantum}. 
Quantum error mitigation protocols improve the performance, but with a sampling overhead that scales exponentially with the circuit depth~\cite{takagi2022fundamental, takagi2023universal}.
Consequently, the $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ circuit depth required by the standard QFT presents significant experimental challenges.


Strategies to mitigate the circuit depth required by the QFT have primarily focused on reducing the hardware execution costs at the cost of only approximating the QFT. These efforts have successfully reduced the depth through techniques such as truncated controlled rotations \cite{Coppersmith2002} and optimized CNOT routing tailored for restricted hardware topologies \cite{park2023reducing}, which approximates the QFT with $\mathcal{O}(n \log n )$ depth. Furthermore dynamic mid-circuit measurements \cite{baumer2024quantum} and quantum circuit cutting \cite{tang2021cutqc, Peng2020} have been employed to trade coherent quantum depth for classical post-processing resources. These optimizations have enabled record experimental QFT fidelities \cite{aumann2026demonstrating}. Despite making the QFT achievable on near-term devices through depth reduction and approximation \cite{Coppersmith2002, park2023reducing}, the approximation methods retain the $\mathcal{O}(n\log n)$ depth. And the protocols based on circuit cutting shift the complexity to the classical domain, requiring exponential classical resources \cite{tang2021cutqc, Peng2020}. This motivates our pursuit of an alternative approach that achieves shallower quantum depth without introducing prohibitive classical post-processing burdens. 






We here accordingly seek to design more shallow alternatives to the QFT. Rather than approximate the QFT we seek to identify key features of the QFT that make it compatible with the HSP.  Fig.~\ref{fig:illus}(a) summarizes the main idea. We focus on shallow circuits that preserve shift invariance, a key property of the QFT. Shift invariance means that the output of the QFT has computational basis probabilities that are invariant under shifts ($\ket{x}\rightarrow \ket{x+c}$) of the input. Those shifts represent random data obfuscating the information about the generator one is after in the hidden subgroup problem.

We construct a shallow circuit family, called \myqc~circuits, that preserves shift invariance,  whilst retaining information about the generator. These circuits interleave transversal Hadamard (H) gates with $L$ layers of phase (P) blocks.  Within this broader family, \myqc-1 is a particularly shallow instance. \myqc-1 combines shallow depth with retained Fisher information about the hidden subgroup generator, making \myqc-1 a candidate replacement for the QFT. Numerical scaling analysis demonstrates that shallow \myqc~circuits retain substantial--exponentially increasing-- Fisher information $\sim e^{kn}$ albeit with a smaller scaling exponent $k$ relative to the full QFT.

We describe how to implement Shor's algorithm with \myqc-1 circuits. An efficiently scaling deep set~\cite{zaheer2017deep} neural network decodes the measurement outcomes, substituting the standard continued fraction expansion typically used in classical post-processing. We undertake classical numerical simulations of the corresponding quantum algorithm and classical post-processing. The results demonstrate that the HP-1, combined with neural post-processing, successfully achieves factorization.

% Shift invariance is a necessary condition for any unitary used in HSP sampling, as it ensures measurement statistics are independent of the random coset representative and thus retain information about the hidden subgroup $V$. 
We now define shift invariance for HSP sampling and derive a necessary condition that any shift-invariant unitary must satisfy. For a subgroup $V \le G$, we denote the uniform superposition over its elements as
\begin{equation}
    \ket{V} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|V|}} \sum_{v \in V} \ket{v}.
\end{equation}
The unitary must yield identical measurement statistics on all cosets of $V$, a requirement formalized as follows:

\begin{definition}[Shift invariance]
    % Let $T_c\ket{v} = \ket{v + c}$ be the shift operator for $c \in G$. 
    A unitary $U$ acting on the group register is shift-invariant for HSP sampling if
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:shift invariant def}
        \left|\bra{x}U\ket{V}\right|^2
        =
        \left|\bra{x}U \ket{c+V}\right|^2,
        \quad
        \forall x,c \in G,\ \forall V \le G.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

This coherent, information-preserving interference ensures that measurement statistics remain strictly independent of the random coset representative $c$. Therefore, shift invariance is not merely a desirable feature but a necessary requirement for extracting hidden subgroup. 

To identify shift-invariant circuits beyond the QFT, we first establish a necessary condition for shift invariance.
\begin{theorem}[Necessary condition of shift invariance]
\label{thm:necessary_modulus_condition}
    Suppose $U$ is a unitary on the group register that satisfies the shift-invariance condition above. Then, for every $x,y\in G$,
    \begin{equation}
        \left|\bra{x}U\ket{y}\right|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}.
    \end{equation}
    Equivalently, there exist phases $\theta_{xy}\in\mathbb{R}$ such that
    \begin{equation}
        \bra{x}U\ket{y}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}e^{\ii\theta_{xy}}.
    \end{equation}
\end{theorem}

% The proof is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:necessary condition of sinv}. This theorem is useful in two ways. Analytically, it provides a diagnostic criterion for verifying whether a candidate circuit can potentially exhibit shift invariance. Constructively, it offers a direct architectural blueprint: the equal-magnitude condition $\frac{1}{|G|}$ is naturally satisfied by applying transversal Hadamard gates across all qubits, which inherently equalizes the magnitude of the unitary elements. Phase blocks can then be embedded to tune the relative phases $\theta_{xy}$ without violating the magnitude constraint. Guided by this structural insight, we conceptualize a family of quantum circuits composed by interleaving Hadamard gates and phase blocks, which we formally designate as the Hadamard-phase (HP) circuit. 

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch of Theorem~\ref{thm:necessary_modulus_condition}]
The proof hinges on analyzing the shift-invariance condition for the trivial subgroup $V = \{0\}$. For subgroup $V = \{0\}$, the state $\ket{V}$ is the computational basis state $\ket{0}$, and the shifted state is $\ket{c+V} = \ket{c}$. The requirement (Eq.\eqref{eq:shift invariant def}) that $\left|\bra{x}U\ket{0}\right|^2 = \left|\bra{x}U\ket{c}\right|^2$ for all $x, c \in G$ implies that, for any fixed output $x$, the magnitude of every matrix element in the $x$-th row of $U$ must be identical. The special case induce the uniform magnitude $\bra{x}U\ket{y} = 1/\sqrt{|G|} e^{\ii \theta_{xy}}$. The full details of this argument are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:necessary condition of sinv}.
\end{proof}


This theorem serves two key purposes. First, it provides a simple and verifiable criterion for assessing whether a given quantum circuit can exhibit shift invariance. Second, the theorem directly informs a circuit architecture: the uniform-magnitude condition $|\langle x|U|y\rangle| = 1/\sqrt{|G|}$ can be naturally realized by applying Hadamard gates to all qubits, which yields a unitary with flat amplitude magnitudes. Phase blocks can be employed to adjust the relative phases $\theta_{xy}$ while preserving this magnitude constraint. This observation motivates the design of the Hadamard-phase (HP) circuit, which alternates layers of Hadamard transforms with parameterized phase blocks.

\section{L-phase-block Hadamard-Phase circuits (\myqc): A shift invariant circuit family}
As illustrated in the upper subfigre of Fig.~\ref{fig:illus} (b), the \myqc-$L$ circuit is structured as an alternating sequence of Hadamard gates and phase blocks. We restrict that there is only one Hadamard gate on each qubit, and there is only one control phase gate on each qubits pair.


We illustrate one layer of the \myqc~circuit. In layer \(l\), which consists of a Hadamard sublayer followed by a phase sublayer, Hadamard gates are applied to the qubits in \(\Lambda_l\), whereas the qubits in \(\bar{\Lambda}_l\) are not
acted on by Hadamard gates at this stage. The subsequent controlled-phase
gates in this layer act only on pairs \((j,k)\) satisfying
\(j\in\Lambda_l\) and \(k\in\bar{\Lambda}_l\). Thus, each such gate
connects one qubit from \(\Lambda_l\) and one qubit from
\(\bar{\Lambda}_l\), while controlled-phase gates within the same
subset are excluded, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:illus}(b).


Formally, let \(H_i\) denote the Hadamard gate applied to qubit
\(i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}\). For each layer \(l\), let
\(\Lambda_l\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,n\}\) denote the set of qubits on which
Hadamard gates are applied in that layer. These subsets form a partition of the whole qubit index set, $\bigcup_{l=1}^L \Lambda_l = \{1,2,\ldots, n\}$, $\Lambda_l \cap \Lambda_{l'} = \emptyset$, for $l\neq l'$. For each layer \(l\), \(\bar{\Lambda}_l\) denotes the remaining qubit indices after removing all indices that have appeared in
\(\Lambda_1,\ldots,\Lambda_l\), namely
$\bar{\Lambda}_l=
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}\setminus
\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{l}\Lambda_k\right)$.

These subsets are coupled via a controlled-phase block, denoted as $\cpgate{\Lambda_l}{\bar{\Lambda}_l}{\bm{\theta}_{\Lambda_l\bar{\Lambda}_l}}$, which applies pairwise operations $\mathrm{CP}_{jk}(\theta_{jk})$ between each qubit $j \in \Lambda_l$ and $k \in \bar{\Lambda}_l$. The control phase gate $\mathrm{CP}_{jk}(\theta_{jk})$ takes $j$-th as the control qubit, $k$-th qubit as the target qubit, $\theta_{jk}$ as the rotated phase. Building upon these notations, we formally define the circuit architecture:
\begin{definition}[\myqc-$k$ circuit]
    A quantum circuit is classified as an \myqc-L circuit if it is constructed using the following layer-wise architecture
    \begin{equation}
        \uqc{L}({\bm{\theta}}) = \bigotimes_{i \in \Lambda_L} H_{i}\prod_{l=L-1}^{1} \left( \cpgate{\Lambda_l}{\bar{\Lambda}_l}{\bm{\theta}_{\Lambda_l\bar{\Lambda}_l}} \bigotimes_{i \in \Lambda_l} H_{i} \right),
    \end{equation}
    where the controlled-phase block is given by
    \begin{equation}
        \cpgate{\Lambda_l}{\bar{\Lambda}_l}{\bm{\theta}_{\Lambda_l\bar{\Lambda}_l}} = \prod_{j \in \bar{\Lambda}_l} \prod_{k \in \Lambda_l} \mathrm{CP}_{jk}(\theta_{jk}).
    \end{equation}
    The parameter set $\bm{\theta}_{\Lambda_l\bar{\Lambda}_l}$ is the collection $\theta_{jk}$, $j\in \Lambda_l$, $k \in \bar{\Lambda}_l$. 
\end{definition}


    
The \myqc~family extends beyond tensor products of QFTs on different registers. In the following, we show that HP circuits preserves shift invariance. This implies that such invariance is not unique to the QFT, but is a feature that arises in a wider family of quantum circuits. 

% To formally validate the viability of this extended design space, we establish that our specific hardware-aware construction rigorously satisfies this property.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:hp_circuits_are_sinv}
    \myqc-$L$ circuits are shift invariant for $\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch of Theorem~\ref{thm:hp_circuits_are_sinv}]
The \myqc{}-$L$ circuit achieves shift invariance over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^n}$ because its linear phase structure isolates the constant coset shift into an unobservable global phase. Specifically, the circuit's unitary phases are linear in the binary variables (e.g., cross-terms like $x_i y_j$). Because any subgroup over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^n}$ takes the form $V = \langle 2^p \rangle$, a coset state $\ket{c+V}$ differs from $\ket{V}$ only at fixed deterministic bit positions $c_i$. Due to phase linearity, these $c_i$ contributions factorize completely into a pure phase term, rendering the final sampling distribution independent of the coset representative. The full derivation is detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:HPk are sinv}.
\end{proof}

The grouped-phase in \myqc circuits reduces are easier to achieve compared to individual gate execution. This hardware-level efficiency arises because consecutive phase operations can be merged into single continuous pulses across multiple experimental platforms, including linear optics \cite{carolan2015universal, Kok2007}, neutral-atom systems \cite{saffman2010quantum, evered2023high, mohan2025parametrized}, trapped ions \cite{Leibfried2003, Tan2013}, and NMR \cite{vandersypen2004nmr}.


\section{\myqc-0 can be used in HSP over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^n}$}
We gives an analytical solution for HSP over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^n}$ via transversal Hadamard gates. We consider the cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}_{2^n}$ since the group can be naturally represented by computational basis. In this case, each subgroup is generated by a power of two,
\begin{equation}
    V=\{q2^p:q=0,\ldots,2^{n-p}-1\} \leq \mathbb{Z}_{2^n},
\label{eq: hp0 subgroup}
\end{equation}
where \(p\) specifies the subgroup generator \(2^p\). Applying a transversal Hadamard layer to a coset state gives
\begin{equation}
    H^{\otimes n}\ket{c+V}
    \propto
    \sum_{k\in\{0,1\}^{p}}(-1)^{k\cdot c}\ket{0}^{\otimes(n-p)}\ket{k}.
    \label{eq: hp0 after th}
\end{equation}
By measuring the state $H^{\otimes n}\ket{c+V}$, one observes that the first $n-p$ bits are always fixed to 0, while the remaining $p$ bits are uniformly distributed over $\{0,1\}^p$. Thus, the value of $p$ can be determined by identifying which bit positions are consistently zero. The detailed calculation and sampling algorithm are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:transversal_hadamard_sampling}.

However, the $\mathbb{Z}_{2^n}$ case represents an instructive but restricted regime. In practical scenarios, one typically aims to solve the HSP over the infinite group $\mathbb{Z}$ using a finite register of $n$ qubits, which can only approximate the problem by encoding $2^n$ elements to recover a subgroup such as $\mathbb{Z}_q$. Under these demanding conditions, the sampled state is no longer an exact subgroup state of the truncated register. Consequently, the idealized structure in Eq. \eqref{eq: hp0 subgroup} no longer holds, so \myqc-0 is generally insufficient and deeper HP circuits are needed.

This motivates the extension from \myqc-0 to deeper members of the \myqc~family, which are designed to preserve shift-invariant sampling structure while allowing greater expressive power than a single Hadamard layer.

Rigorously, we use the discrete Fisher information (DFI) as a natural metric of the distinguishability.
\begin{equation}
    \fii(r, n) = \sum_{x = 0}^{2^n -1} \frac{\left(\text{Pr}(x\mid r+1) - \text{Pr}(x\mid r)\right)^2}{\text{Pr}(x\mid r)},
\end{equation}
where the $x$ represent measurement outcome and $r$ is the hidden subgroup generator, and $\text{Pr}(x\mid r)$ is the probability of measuring $x$ when the subgroup generator is $r$. The sample complexity for estimating a discrete parameter is formally governed by the Hammersley-Chapman-Robbins bound \cite{chapman1951minimum, hammersley1950estimating},
% \begin{equation}

For standard QFT, we can express the DFI as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:qft dfi}
    \fii_{\mathrm{QFT}}(r,n) \approx \frac{4\pi^2}{9}\left(\frac{2^{2n}}{r^2}-1\right) \sim \mathcal{O}\left(2^{2n}\right).
\end{equation}
The calculation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:qft dfi} is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:qft_fisher_information}.

\section{Efficient Neural network for classical post-processing}
To bypass the exponential overhead of evaluating full probability distributions, we employ a polynomial-scaling Deep Sets neural network \cite{zaheer2017deep, edwards2016towards} to predict the hidden subgroup generator directly from the raw measurement outcomes $\{b_i\}$. The input data is generated using the optimized \myqc{}-1 circuit evaluated in Fig.~\ref{fig:fi_scaling}. As depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:recovery_pipeline}(a), a shared feature map $\phi$ processes $m$ bitstrings to compute an aggregated, permutation-invariant representation $\sum_i\phi(b_i)$. Subsequently, a beam-search decoder \cite{sutskever2014sequence, wiseman2016sequence} performs a heuristic top-$k$ search to rank candidate periods. By maintaining a restricted set of probable solutions, this architecture avoids exponential sample-space computations and outputs a ranked list of candidate generators for classical verification.

\section{Replacing \(\mathcal{O}(n^2)\) QFT with \(\mathcal{O}(n)\) HP-1 in Shor's Algorithm}
This experiment is intended to test the practical recoverability of subgroup information, rather than only reporting information-theoretic quantities in the abstract. In the context of Shor's algorithm, this inherently translates to an order-finding problem, which seeks the smallest positive integer $r$ such that
$a^r \equiv 1 \pmod{N}$.
To ensure compatibility with the finite capacity of the quantum register, the base $a$ is suitably chosen so that the resulting order $r$ naturally falls within the range $1 \le r \le 2^n$. This methodological choice guarantees that the target period is fully resolvable by the $n$-qubit system while faithfully retaining the core structural complexity of the task. Fig.~\ref{fig:recovery_pipeline}(b) shows the training behavior for 9-, 10-, and 11-qubit instances. The loss decreases rapidly, while both the top-1 and top-3 accuracies increase within a modest number of epochs. Here, top-1 accuracy denotes the probability that the correct period is the first predicted candidate, whereas top-3 accuracy denotes the probability that the correct period appears among the first three candidates. These results indicate that the measurement distributions produced by shallow HP circuits retain learnable period-dependent features.

We further evaluate the recovery procedure on Shor-type period-finding instances, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:recovery_pipeline}(c). Each entry corresponds to a composite integer to be factorized, and the color records the minimum number \(k\) of HP-\(1\)-based guesses required for successful period recovery. Most instances are solved with \(k=1\), meaning that the correct period is obtained from the first guess, while only a small fraction require \(k=2\) or \(k=3\). This suggests that \myqc-1 measurement data already provide effective information for period recovery, and that a small number of ranked guesses can extend the coverage to more challenging instances. Overall, the results show that an efficient classical decoder can successfully exploit the usable structure preserved by the shallow HP-circuit measurements.

To ensure practical viability, we evaluate the previously trained \myqc-1  neural network decoder under a post-circuit global depolarizing channel, which simulates environmental noise corrupting the final quantum state. We quantify this with a noise strength parameter $\eta$, where $\eta=1$ represents complete signal loss. The held-out recovery accuracy remains highly robust and essentially unchanged up to a moderate noise level of $\eta \approx 0.215$. As the noise increases further, performance shows a marked drop: at $\eta=0.464$, the accuracy decreases to $0.491$ for $9$ qubits and $0.881$ for $10$ qubits. Ultimately, at the fully depolarizing point ($\eta=1$), all subgroup information is erased entirely, causing the model's predictive accuracy to correctly fall to chance-level guessing. Detailed settings, complete numerical results, and the corresponding interpretation of these scaling differences are given in Appendix \ref{app:noise_robustness_details}.

\section{Summary and outlook}
We propose a hardware-efficient framework for solving the HSP by shallow, shift-invariant \myqc{}-1 circuits with a polynomial-scaling neural network. To verify the scalability of this protocol, we analyzed the growth model of the DFI. Finally, we evaluated our protocol on Shor's algorithm. The numerical simulations demonstrate that our approach successfully recovers the hidden subgroup with high accuracy, while remaining robust against moderate depolarizing noise.

Realizing a practical HSP protocol requires an optimized oracle $U_f$ implementations. Current strategies to mitigate the cost of $U_f$ include $\mathcal{O}(1)$-query classical pre-training of diagonal Hamiltonians \cite{learning_hamiltonian_2025}, space-time tradeoffs for non-Clifford gate reductions \cite{yu2025back}, and automatic oracle synthesis frameworks \cite{aos_qdk_2024}.
