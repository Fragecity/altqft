\begin{abstract}
The Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT) is required by hidden subgroup algorithms including Shor's algorithm for factoring. The circuit depth and long-range gates of the QFT remains challenging for near-term hardware. To find more shall alternatives we identify two functional properties that are exploited by the QFT to enable HSP. Firstly the shift invariance property of the QFT allows for the removal of a random overall shift. Secondly, the QFT retains information about the hidden subgroup generator accessible in the computational basis. We quantify that information via the discrete Fisher information. We construct a family of shallow circuits using Hadamards and controlled-Phase gates, HP-L circuits, that preserve shift invariance. Numerical analysis shows these circuits retain Fisher information about the generator with the same scaling as the QFT.  Numerical experiments with the $\mathcal{O}(n)$ HP-1 replacing the $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ QFT demonstrates successful Shor factoring, using an efficiently scaling neural network for classical post-processing. 
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction} The $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ circuit depth required by the standard Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT) presents experimental challenges. The QFT is used in the exponential quantum speedups of landmark protocols—such as Shor's algorithms for prime factorization and discrete logarithms \cite{Shor1994, Shor1997}, as well as Simon's algorithm \cite{Simon1997}. The associated family of algorithms is known as the (quantum) hidden subgroup problem (HSP) algorithms \cite{Kitaev1995, Jozsa2001, Lomont2004}. 
The $\mathcal{O}(n^{2})$ depth complexity of the QFT remains a critical bottleneck for implementing HSP algorithms in hardware. For a $100$-qubit system ($n=100$), the QFT demands thousands of circuit layers, which vastly exceeds current hardware capabilities. For instance, IBM's $127$-qubit Eagle processor is limited to a depth of $60$ layers even with extensive error mitigation \cite{kim2023evidence}, and Google's $105$-qubit Willow processor reaches its operational threshold at around $40$ layers~\cite{google2025quantum}. 
Quantum error mitigation protocols improve the performance, but with a sampling overhead that scales exponentially with the circuit depth~\cite{takagi2022fundamental, takagi2023universal}.

We now define shift invariance for HSP sampling and derive a necessary condition that any shift-invariant unitary must satisfy. For a subgroup $V \le G$, we denote the uniform superposition over its elements as $\ket{V} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|V|}} \sum_{v \in V} \ket{v}$.
%The unitary must yield identical measurement statistics on all cosets of $V$, a requirement formalized as follows:

\begin{definition}[Shift invariance]
    % Let $T_c\ket{v} = \ket{v + c}$ be the shift operator for $c \in G$. 
    A unitary $U$ acting on the group register is shift-invariant for HSP sampling if
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:shift invariant def}
        \left|\bra{x}U\ket{V}\right|^2
        =
        \left|\bra{x}U \ket{c+V}\right|^2,
        \quad
        \forall x,c \in G,\ \forall V \le G.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

This coherent, information-preserving interference ensures that measurement statistics remain strictly independent of the random coset representative $c$. Therefore, shift invariance is not merely a desirable feature but a necessary requirement for extracting hidden subgroup. 

To identify shift-invariant circuits beyond the QFT, we first establish a necessary condition for shift invariance.
\begin{proposition}[Necessary condition of shift invariance]
\label{thm:necessary_modulus_condition}
    Suppose $U$ is a unitary on the group register that satisfies the shift-invariance condition above. Then, for every $x,y\in G$ there exist phases $\theta_{xy}\in\mathbb{R}$ such that
%    \begin{equation}
        %\left|\bra{x}U\ket{y}\right|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}.
    %\end{equation}
    \begin{equation}
        \bra{x}U\ket{y}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}e^{\ii\theta_{xy}}.
    \end{equation}
\end{proposition}

% The proof is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:necessary condition of sinv}. This theorem is useful in two ways. Analytically, it provides a diagnostic criterion for verifying whether a candidate circuit can potentially exhibit shift invariance. Constructively, it offers a direct architectural blueprint: the equal-magnitude condition $\frac{1}{|G|}$ is naturally satisfied by applying transversal Hadamard gates across all qubits, which inherently equalizes the magnitude of the unitary elements. Phase blocks can then be embedded to tune the relative phases $\theta_{xy}$ without violating the magnitude constraint. Guided by this structural insight, we conceptualize a family of quantum circuits composed by interleaving Hadamard gates and phase blocks, which we formally designate as the Hadamard-phase (HP) circuit. 

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch of Proposition~\ref{thm:necessary_modulus_condition}]
The proof hinges on analyzing the shift-invariance condition for the trivial subgroup $V = \{0\}$. For subgroup $V = \{0\}$, the state $\ket{V}$ is the computational basis state $\ket{0}$, and the shifted state is $\ket{c+V} = \ket{c}$. The requirement (Eq.\eqref{eq:shift invariant def}) that $\left|\bra{x}U\ket{0}\right|^2 = \left|\bra{x}U\ket{c}\right|^2$ for all $x, c \in G$ implies that, for any fixed output $x$, the magnitude of every matrix element in the $x$-th row of $U$ must be identical. The special case induce the uniform magnitude $\bra{x}U\ket{y} = 1/\sqrt{|G|} e^{\ii \theta_{xy}}$. The full details of this argument are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:necessary condition of sinv}.
\end{proof}

\section{L-phase-block Hadamard-Phase circuits (\myqc): A shift invariant circuit family}
Proposition \ref{thm:necessary_modulus_condition} suggests a simple design principle for constructing shift-invariant circuits. The condition \(|\langle x|U|y\rangle|=1/\sqrt{|G|}\) requires flat amplitude magnitudes, which are naturally generated by Hadamard gates. The remaining freedom lies in the relative phases \(\theta_{xy}\), which can be adjusted by controlled phase gates ($\mathrm{CP}(\theta)=\ket{00}\bra{00}+\ket{01}\bra{01}+\ket{10}\bra{10}+\exp(\ii\theta)\ket{11}\bra{11}$) without changing these magnitudes. This motivates the Hadamard-phase (HP) circuit family we shall now introduce. As illustrated in the upper subfigure of Fig.~\ref{fig:illus} (b), the \myqc-$L$ circuit is structured as an alternating sequence of Hadamard gates and phase blocks. To respect shift invariance (see Theorem \ref{thm:hp_circuits_are_sinv} below) we enforce three key rules (i) each qubit is acted on by a Hadamard gate exactly once, and (ii) each qubit pair is connected by at most one controlled-phase gate, (iii) each controlled phase gate must come together with 2 Hadamards, one before and one after the phase gate. Here, ``once'' refers to the entire $L$-layer circuit rather than to a single layer. Examples of valid circuits are illustrated in Fig.\ref{fig:illus}

In-between the Hadamard layers the two sets are coupled via a controlled-phase block, denoted as $\cpgate{\Lambda_l}{\bar{\Lambda}_l}{\bm{\theta}_{\Lambda_l\bar{\Lambda}_l}}$, which applies pairwise operations $\mathrm{CP}_{jk}(\theta_{jk})$ between each qubit $j \in \Lambda_l$ and $k \in \bar{\Lambda}_l$. Building upon those notations, we formally define the circuit architecture:
\begin{definition}[\myqc-$L$ circuit]
    A quantum circuit is classified as an \myqc-L circuit if it is constructed using the following layer-wise architecture
    \begin{equation}
        \uqc{L}({\bm{\theta}}) = \bigotimes_{i \in \Lambda_L} H_{i}\prod_{l=L-1}^{1} \left( \cpgate{\Lambda_l}{\bar{\Lambda}_l}{\bm{\theta}_{\Lambda_l\bar{\Lambda}_l}} \bigotimes_{i \in \Lambda_l} H_{i} \right),
    \end{equation}
    where the controlled-phase block is given by
    \begin{equation}
        \cpgate{\Lambda_l}{\bar{\Lambda}_l}{\bm{\theta}_{\Lambda_l\bar{\Lambda}_l}} = \prod_{j \in \bar{\Lambda}_l} \prod_{k \in \Lambda_l} \mathrm{CP}_{jk}(\theta_{jk}).
    \end{equation}
    The parameter set $\bm{\theta}_{\Lambda_l\bar{\Lambda}_l}$ is the collection $\theta_{jk}$, $j\in \Lambda_l$, $k \in \bar{\Lambda}_l$. 
\end{definition}


    
In the following, we show that HP circuits preserves shift invariance. This implies that such invariance is not unique to the QFT, but is a feature that arises in a wider family of quantum circuits.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:hp_circuits_are_sinv}
    \myqc-$L$ circuits are shift invariant for $\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch of Theorem~\ref{thm:hp_circuits_are_sinv}]
The \myqc{}-$L$ circuit achieves shift invariance over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^n}$ because its linear phase structure isolates the constant coset shift into an unobservable global phase. Specifically, the circuit's unitary phases are linear in the binary variables (e.g., cross-terms like $x_i y_j$). Because any subgroup over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^n}$ takes the form $V = \langle 2^p \rangle$, a coset state $\ket{c+V}$ differs from $\ket{V}$ only at fixed deterministic bit positions $c_i$. Due to phase linearity, these $c_i$ contributions factorize completely into a pure phase term, rendering the final sampling distribution independent of the coset representative. The full derivation is detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:HPk are sinv}.
\end{proof}


\section{\myqc-0 can be used in HSP over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^n}$}
We gives an analytical solution for HSP over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^n}$ via transversal Hadamard gates. We consider the cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}_{2^n}$ since the group can be naturally represented by computational basis. In this case, each subgroup is generated by a power of two,
\begin{equation}
    V=\{q2^p:q=0,\ldots,2^{n-p}-1\} \leq \mathbb{Z}_{2^n},
\label{eq: hp0 subgroup}
\end{equation}
where \(p\) specifies the subgroup generator \(2^p\). Applying a transversal Hadamard layer to a coset state gives
\begin{equation}
    H^{\otimes n}\ket{c+V}
    \propto
    \sum_{k\in\{0,1\}^{p}}(-1)^{k\cdot c}\ket{0}^{\otimes(n-p)}\ket{k}.
    \label{eq: hp0 after th}
\end{equation}
By measuring the state $H^{\otimes n}\ket{c+V}$, one observes that the first $n-p$ bits are always fixed to 0, while the remaining $p$ bits are uniformly distributed over $\{0,1\}^p$. Thus, the value of $p$ can be determined by identifying which bit positions are consistently zero. The detailed calculation and sampling algorithm are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:transversal_hadamard_sampling}.

Under these demanding conditions, the sampled state is no longer an exact subgroup state of the truncated register. Consequently, the idealized structure in Eq. \eqref{eq: hp0 subgroup} no longer holds, so \myqc-0 is generally insufficient and deeper HP circuits are needed.

Rigorously, we use the discrete Fisher information (DFI) as a metric of the distinguishability.
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:DFI}
    \fii(r, n) = \sum_{x = 0}^{2^n -1} \frac{\left(\text{Pr}(x\mid r+1) - \text{Pr}(x\mid r)\right)^2}{\text{Pr}(x\mid r)},
\end{equation}
where the $x$ represent measurement outcome and $r$ is the hidden subgroup generator, and $\text{Pr}(x\mid r)$ is the probability of measuring $x$ when the subgroup generator is $r$. The sample complexity for estimating a discrete parameter is formally governed by the DFI (Eq.\eqref{eq:DFI}) via the Hammersley-Chapman-Robbins bound~\cite{chapman1951minimum, hammersley1950estimating}, 
\begin{equation}
    m \ge \frac{\ln\left( 1 + \epsilon^{-2} \right)}{\ln\left( 1 + \fii(r) \right)},
\end{equation}
where $m$ is the required number of samples. The bound guarantees that retaining an exponentially growing DFI results in tractable sample complexity for the subsequent neural-network-based post processing.

\section{Efficient Neural network for classical post-processing}
To bypass the exponential overhead of evaluating full probability distributions, we employ a polynomial-scaling Deep Sets neural network \cite{zaheer2017deep, edwards2016towards} to predict the hidden subgroup generator directly from the raw measurement outcomes $\{b_i\}$. The input data is generated using the optimized \myqc{}-1 circuit evaluated in Fig.~\ref{fig:fi_scaling}. As depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:recovery_pipeline}(a), a shared feature map $\phi$ processes $m$ bitstrings to compute an aggregated, permutation-invariant representation $\sum_i\phi(b_i)$. Subsequently, a beam-search decoder \cite{sutskever2014sequence, wiseman2016sequence} performs a heuristic top-$k$ search to rank candidate periods. By maintaining a restricted set of probable solutions, this architecture avoids exponential sample-space computations and outputs a ranked list of candidate generators for classical verification.

\section{Replacing \texorpdfstring{$\mathcal{O}(n^2)$}{O(n2)} QFT with \texorpdfstring{$\mathcal{O}(n)$}{O(n)} \myqc{}-1 in Shor's Algorithm}
We evaluate the optimized \myqc{}-1 circuit on Shor's algorithm, demonstrating that its shallow circuit measurements retain learnable features for successful classical post processing. Shor's Algorithm seeks the generator $r$ satisfying $a^r \equiv 1 \pmod{N}$. We select the base $a$ such that $1 \le r \le 2^{n/2}$, ensuring resolvability by the $n$-qubit register. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:recovery_pipeline}(b), the neural network achieves rapid convergence on 9-, 10-, and 11-qubit instances. Both the top-1 (correct period ranked first) and top-3 accuracies increase swiftly within a few epochs.
% Fig.~\ref{fig:recovery_pipeline}(b) shows the training behavior for 9-, 10-, and 11-qubit instances. The loss decreases rapidly, while both the top-1 and top-3 accuracies increase within a modest number of epochs. Here, top-1 accuracy denotes the probability that the correct period is the first predicted candidate, whereas top-3 accuracy denotes the probability that the correct period appears among the first three candidates. These results indicate that the measurement distributions produced by shallow HP circuits retain learnable period-dependent features.

Our evaluation on Shor factoring demonstrates that our protocol can find the subgroup generator. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:recovery_pipeline}(c), each composite integer is colored by the minimum number of guesses $k$. Most instances are solved on the first attempt ($k=1$), indicating the raw measurement distribution provides sufficient information for period extraction. 

Our evaluation under a post-circuit global depolarizing channel demonstrates that the trained \myqc{}-1 neural decoder is robust against moderate environmental noise. Parameterizing the noise strength by $\eta$, the held-out recovery accuracy remains stable up to $\eta \approx 0.215$. Performance degrades at higher noise levels, dropping to $0.491$ for $9$ qubits and $0.881$ for $10$ qubits at $\eta=0.464$. At the fully depolarizing limit ($\eta=1$), the subgroup information is erased, reducing the model to chance-level guessing. Appendix~\ref{app:noise_robustness_details} provides detailed settings, complete numerical results, and corresponding interpretations.

\section{Summary and outlook}
We propose a hardware-efficient framework for solving the HSP by shallow, shift-invariant \myqc{}-1 circuits with a polynomial-scaling neural network. To verify the scalability of this protocol, we analyzed the growth model of the DFI. Finally, we evaluated our protocol on Shor's algorithm. The numerical simulations demonstrate that our approach successfully recovers the hidden subgroup with high accuracy, while remaining robust against moderate depolarizing noise.

Realizing a practical HSP protocol requires an optimized oracle $U_f$ implementations. Current strategies to mitigate the cost of $U_f$ include $\mathcal{O}(1)$-query classical pre-training of diagonal Hamiltonians \cite{learning_hamiltonian_2025}, space-time tradeoffs for non-Clifford gate reductions \cite{yu2025back}, and automatic oracle synthesis frameworks \cite{aos_qdk_2024}. 


Apart from the $\mathcal{O}(n)$ scaling being hardware friendly,  the grouped phase gates in \myqc circuits reduces are easier to achieve compared to individual phase gate execution. This hardware-level efficiency arises because consecutive phase operations can be merged into single continuous pulses across multiple experimental platforms, including in linear optics \cite{carolan2015universal, Kok2007}, neutral-atom systems \cite{saffman2010quantum, evered2023high, mohan2025parametrized}, trapped ions \cite{Leibfried2003, Tan2013}, and NMR \cite{vandersypen2004nmr}.
